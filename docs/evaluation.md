# Evaluation — honest metrics

> **Generated by `python -m src.model.full_report` on 2026-09-01.** Every number on this page comes out of that command; none is typed by hand. Re-run it and the page rewrites itself.

Data: 20,000 orders, synthetic — causal COD/RTO generator (seed 42). Split chronological; the test split is never resampled — train/val/test = 14000/3000/3000, test RTO rate 16.9% (natural, never resampled). Label-derived history features respect a **7-day outcome-availability lag**.

## 1. Why accuracy is not on this page

At a 16.9% RTO rate, a model that flags nothing scores 83.1% accuracy and prevents zero returns. Accuracy would reward exactly the model we must not ship, so it is not reported anywhere in this repository. The headline is the rupee cost curve.

## 4. The money story

Costs are example-dependent: a missed return on a ₹3,000 order hurts more than on a ₹300 one. `c_FN` = round-trip logistics and handling plus a share of the order value; `c_FP` = the servicing and abandonment cost of putting a genuine customer through friction. Both are assumptions, stated here and swept in §8.

| policy | ₹ per 1,000 orders |
|---|---:|
| Approve everything | ₹64,795 |
| Block all COD (naive) | ₹71,776 |
| **Axiom @ frozen τ** | **₹51,199** |

Axiom is ₹20,578 per 1,000 orders cheaper than blocking all COD (95% CI ₹14,404–₹25,979) and 21.0% cheaper than approving everything. Note the middle row: **blocking all COD costs more than doing nothing at all.** That is the false-positive argument in rupees — the friction you inflict on good customers is not free, and a blunt policy spends more of it than the fraud is worth.

## 5. Is the machine learning worth it?

Beating "do nothing" proves nothing. The real question is whether the model beats what a competent risk analyst would ship without it. Every contender below gets identical features and splits, isotonic calibration on validation, and its own validation-fitted threshold.

| model | τ (val) | PR-AUC | ROC-AUC | Brier | ₹ / 1k |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| prevalence (no skill) | 0.195 | — | — | 0.1413 | ₹64,795 |
| rules-only scorecard | 0.265 | 0.455 | 0.786 | 0.1148 | ₹52,276 |
| logistic regression | 0.270 | 0.506 | 0.798 | 0.1096 | ₹50,171 |
| **LightGBM (Axiom)** | 0.315 | 0.509 | 0.800 | 0.1078 | ₹51,199 |

Paired bootstrap on the gap (same resampled orders for both models):

- vs rules-only scorecard: PR-AUC gain [+0.032, +0.077] → **LightGBM wins**.
- vs logistic regression: PR-AUC gain [-0.010, +0.017] → **not shown to be better** — the interval spans zero.

We report this as it came out. The tree ensemble earns a clear win over the expert scorecard, but against a calibrated logistic regression the gap is inside the noise on this dataset. LightGBM stays in the pipeline because it handles the categorical and interaction structure the ring/velocity features carry and because SHAP gives per-order attributions the agent can narrate — not because we have shown it is significantly more accurate here.

## 6. Which good customers pay for the false positives?

A portfolio-level false-positive rate hides the part that matters. `fp_rate_on_good` is the share of *genuine* customers in a slice that were put through friction.

| slice | n | good customers | wrongly challenged | rate | ₹ cost | recall there |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| order_value: **₹2k–5k** | 282 | 214 | 31 | 14.5% | ₹10,434 | 0.40 |
| buyer: **first-time** | 480 | 348 | 41 | 11.8% | ₹7,594 | 0.60 |
| category: **electronics** | 412 | 327 | 38 | 11.6% | ₹12,641 | 0.32 |
| city_tier: **tier 3** | 762 | 589 | 64 | 10.9% | ₹11,133 | 0.39 |
| address: **incomplete address** | 1060 | 813 | 80 | 9.8% | ₹13,721 | 0.52 |

The friction is not spread evenly. Within **payment**, a genuine customer in *COD* (9.4%) is far more likely to be challenged than one in *prepaid* (0.6%) — 16.2×.

| dimension | most challenged | least challenged | ratio |
|---|---|---|---:|
| payment | COD (9.4%) | prepaid (0.6%) | 16.2× |
| order_value | ₹2k–5k (14.5%) | <₹500 (2.8%) | 5.1× |
| category | electronics (11.6%) | books (2.3%) | 5.0× |
| city_tier | tier 3 (10.9%) | tier 1 (2.9%) | 3.8× |
| address | incomplete address (9.8%) | complete address (3.8%) | 2.6× |
| buyer | first-time (11.8%) | returning (4.8%) | 2.5× |

This is why the response is **dynamic friction and not a block**: a mis-flagged customer is asked to confirm an address or offered a prepaid link, and can clear themselves in one step. The disparity above is a real cost we own and monitor, not one we discovered in the writeup and left out. It uses no protected attribute — city tier, order value and category are commercial variables — so this is an operational harm audit, not a legal fairness audit.

## 7. Two leakage taxes we paid

**Outcome-availability lag.** An order placed today cannot know whether yesterday's order was returned — the delivery attempt has not resolved. Label-derived history features are therefore held back 7 days.

| encoder | test PR-AUC | ROC-AUC |
|---|---:|---:|
| naive as-of (0-day lag, optimistic) | 0.5116 | 0.7992 |
| **shipped (7-day lag)** | **0.5089** | 0.8001 |

Honouring the lag costs us 0.0027 PR-AUC. Small — most of the signal is structural (address quality, payment mode, distance) rather than recent-outcome memory — but it is the difference between a number that survives production and one that does not.

**Deliberate leakage.** Fitting the target encoders on all rows, including each row's own label, produces the sort of figure public RTO models advertise:

| model | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Axiom (honest)** | **0.800** | **0.509** |
| Leaked variant — INVALID | 0.966 | 0.841 |

We can manufacture the impressive number in one line of code. It is worthless: the encoder has memorised the answer. The lower number is the one that would survive contact with real orders.

## 8. Where the band cut-points come from

GREEN / AMBER / RED are not hand-picked. Each band's action has a friction cost and an efficacy, and setting the expected costs equal gives closed-form cut-points (`src/model/threshold.py`):

`green < 0.262 ≤ amber < 0.499 ≤ red`

| policy | ₹ / 1k | green | amber | red |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Derived from the cost model** | ₹58,811 | 2359 | 504 | 137 |
| Previously hard-coded 0.15 / 0.45 | ₹60,569 | 1756 | 1107 | 137 |

Deriving the cut-points instead of guessing them is worth ₹1,758 per 1,000 orders.

The efficacies (`amber 0.35`, `red 0.90`) are **assumptions** — we have no counterfactual data on what a step-up prevents. So they are swept rather than asserted:

- τ_low ranges **0.11–0.73** and τ_high **0.41–1.00** across the plausible range.
- The amber cut-point is dominated by the assumed step-up efficacy. Any merchant deploying this should measure that efficacy on their own traffic and re-derive; the formula, not the constant, is the deliverable.

## 9. Known limitations

- **The data is synthetic.** No clean public Indian COD/RTO dataset exists, so orders are drawn from an explicit causal model of the published RTO drivers. Every metric here is real, measured on held-out data the model never saw — but the *world* is generated. On real orders the model must be recalibrated before any rupee claim.
- **The cost model is assumed, not audited.** The rupee conclusions move with `c_FP`/`c_FN`; the interactive slider in the dashboard exists so a reviewer can substitute their own numbers.
- **The action efficacies are assumed** (§8) and are the largest source of uncertainty in the band economics.
- **LightGBM is not shown to beat logistic regression** on this dataset (§5).
- **A calibrated ranker is not a fraud oracle.** Recall at the frozen threshold is 33%: most returns are not caught, and the system is built around that fact — bounded, reversible friction with human override, rather than blocks.
